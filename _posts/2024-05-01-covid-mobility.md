<p class="eyebrow">Project</p>
<h1 class="page-title">Human mobility during COVID-19 across socioeconomic groups</h1>
<p class="page-intro">A census tract–level comparison of San Diego County and New York City, examining not just how much mobility fell during the pandemic, but where it fell and for whom.</p>

<h2 class="legend-head">The question</h2>

<p>Many studies documented that people moved less during COVID-19. Far fewer specified <em>where</em> those changes happened, or how they tracked with socioeconomic conditions. Aggregate mobility declines can hide the fact that some neighborhoods stopped moving almost entirely while others barely changed.</p>

<h2 class="legend-head">Approach</h2>

<p>I combined mobile phone mobility data with U.S. Census socioeconomic indicators at the census tract level. Latent profile analysis grouped tracts into socioeconomic classes; network science measures then described how each tract sat within the wider mobility network — whether it acted as a hub, a bridge, or a periphery, before and during the pandemic.</p>

<h2 class="legend-head">What we found</h2>

<p>Urban parks and rural areas containing national parks became significant mobility hubs during the pandemic. At the same time, affluent neighborhoods with high educational attainment saw their network centrality decline. The result was a substantial reshuffling of urban mobility structure, one that widened existing socioeconomic differences in movement and in access to resources.</p>
